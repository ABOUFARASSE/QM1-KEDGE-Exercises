LEVEL 1: RECOGNITION

Statistical vocabulary

A survey asks 240 first-year students about programme, transport mode, satisfaction (1–5), number of absences and commuting time in minutes.

  1. Identify the population, statistical unit and five variables.

  2. Classify every variable as nominal, ordinal, discrete or continuous.

  3. Give one possible value for each variable.

Identify the statistical concept, state the relevant formula or rule, calculate carefully, and finish with an interpretation in context.

Detailed solution

Method. The population is the complete group addressed by the research question; the unit is one entity on which observations are recorded. A variable is classified from the meaning of its values, not from the way it is coded.

Result. Population: the 240 surveyed first-year students, if the objective is to describe those respondents. Unit: one student. Programme and transport mode are qualitative nominal variables because their categories have no natural order. Satisfaction is qualitative ordinal because 1 to 5 rank levels but gaps are not guaranteed equal. Number of absences is quantitative discrete because it is counted. Commuting time is quantitative continuous because time can, in principle, take any value within an interval.

Interpretation. A numerical code does not automatically make a variable quantitative: satisfaction coded 1–5 remains ordinal.

LEVEL 2: GUIDED REASONING

Population, sample and bias

KEDGE wants the average commuting time of all 1,200 first-year students. An online questionnaire is posted at 8 a.m.; 180 volunteers answer before noon.

  1. Define the target population and sample.

  2. Is the sample necessarily representative? Identify two plausible biases.

  3. Propose a better sampling design.

Identify the statistical concept, state the relevant formula or rule, calculate carefully, and finish with an interpretation in context.

Detailed solution

Method. Separate the group about which the school wants a conclusion from the group actually observed. Representativeness depends on the selection mechanism, not simply on sample size.

Result. The target population is all 1,200 first-year students; the sample contains the 180 volunteers. Voluntary response can over-represent highly engaged students, and the morning time window can under-represent students commuting or attending class. A better design is a random sample drawn from the student list, possibly stratified by programme or campus, followed by reminders and a non-response analysis.

Interpretation. A sample of 180 can still be biased. Random selection and coverage matter more than the raw response count.

Build a frequency distribution

The numbers of purchases made by 20 customers are: 0, 1, 2, 1, 3, 2, 2, 4, 1, 0, 2, 3, 1, 2, 5, 3, 2, 1, 4, 2.

  1. Construct counts, relative frequencies, cumulative counts and cumulative frequencies.

  2. Find the share with at most two purchases.

  3. Choose an appropriate graph and justify it.

LEVEL 4: SYNTHESIS

Dashboard audit

A manager reports that satisfaction “doubled” because a truncated-axis chart rises from 3.8 to 4.2, and claims the sample represents the school although only members of one student association answered. List four statistical problems, redesign the communication and write a defensible conclusion.

Identify the statistical concept, state the relevant formula or rule, calculate carefully, and finish with an interpretation in context.

Detailed solution

Method. Audit the sampling frame, scale, numerical claim and scope of inference.

Result. First, 4.2 is not twice 3.8; the increase is (4.2/3.8−1)×100=10.53%(4.2/3.8-1)\times100=10.53\%. Second, a truncated axis visually magnifies the change. Third, association members form a convenience sample. Fourth, an ordinal average should be accompanied by the full distribution. Use a clearly labelled scale from 1 to 5, show category percentages, disclose nn and the recruitment method, and avoid generalising beyond respondents.

Interpretation. A defensible statement is: “Among responding association members, mean reported satisfaction rose from 3.8 to 4.2; this 0.4-point increase cannot automatically be generalised to all students.”
